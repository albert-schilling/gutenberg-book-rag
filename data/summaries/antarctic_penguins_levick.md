Antarctic Penguins: A Study of Their Social Habits is a natural‑history field report by G. M. Levick, written from his time with the Discovery Expedition. The book opens with a broad introduction to the ancient lineage of penguins and their sea‑adapted anatomy, then moves through a series of observational chapters that follow a single Adelie rookery from early arrival to final departure. Levick describes the geography of the colonies (Cape Adare, Duke of York Island), the long treks across sea‑ice, the nightly silence, nest building, egg‑laying schedules and incubation tables, and the coordinated foraging trips of paired birds. Detailed sections record courtship fights, the “ecstatic” post‑fight rituals, and the role of skuas as opportunistic thieves. Later chapters recount the hazards of avalanches, the heavy‑bellied parents feeding chicks, and the eventual exodus of thousands of adults, ending with a solitary, silent beach. An appendix supplies plumage and soft‑part descriptions, while a final note compares the calls of Emperor penguins to a ship’s horn and points readers to Dr. Wilson’s earlier expedition report. The work concludes that Adelie penguins maintain a surprisingly ordered, resilient social system despite extreme Antarctic conditions.
