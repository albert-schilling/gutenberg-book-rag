Robert DeCourcy Ward’s *Climate, Considered Especially in Relation to Man* is a popular‑science synthesis written for educated readers without specialized training. Built from ten years of lecture notes, the book is organized thematically rather than as a textbook, moving from general principles of solar insolation and the division of the globe into tropical, temperate and polar zones, through the physical effects of altitude, humidity, sea‑land breezes and thunderstorms, to the ways climate shapes human life.

The middle sections describe regional climates—mountain, monsoon, steppe, and maritime zones—and their influence on agriculture, disease, settlement patterns, clothing, dwellings and transportation. Ward links atmospheric conditions to the spread of germs, noting that seasonal temperature swings foster respiratory illnesses in winter and diarrhoeal diseases in summer. He also surveys historical impacts of winds, trade winds, monsoons and cyclones on navigation, migration and warfare.

In the final chapters Ward surveys long‑term climate records, citing studies such as Brückner’s 35‑year rainfall cycles and Schott’s temperature series, and concludes that while no steady trend is evident, recognizable oscillations affect crops, water resources, industry and transport. He calls for more data collection and analysis, arguing that understanding these cycles will eventually permit reliable long‑range forecasts of great benefit to humanity.
